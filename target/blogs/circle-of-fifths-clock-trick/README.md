# How to Draw the Circle of Fifths From Memory in Seconds (Using a Simple Clock Trick)

Most guitalele and guitar players treat the Circle of Fifths like a giant table of formulas to memorize. If you don't have a reference chart open in front of you, figuring out which chords belong to an unfamiliar key—like **C# Major**—can bring a practice session to a halt.

You don't actually need to memorize the Circle of Fifths. By picturing a standard 12-hour clock face and remembering just two anchor notes, you can draw the entire circle from scratch in seconds and immediately pull out the scale notes, diatonic chords, and relative minor for any major key.

*(Note: To keep things practical for fretboard navigation, this method uses sharp notation (`#`) across the entire circle rather than switching between sharps and flats.)*

---

## Step 1: Draw the Circle Using the 12-Hour Clock Trick

Start by drawing a circle (or a 12-sided polygon) and marking the 12 clock positions around the perimeter (`1` through `12`).

### 1. Place Your Two Anchor Notes

Memorize only two spots:

* **12 o'clock:** **C**
* **1 o'clock:** **G**

![Circle of Fifths on a 12-hour clock](https://palash90.github.io/site-assets/blogs/circle-of-fifths-clock-trick/step1.png "Circle of Fifths on a 12-hour clock")

### 2. Fill in the Odd Positions (Whole Steps From G)

Moving clockwise around the odd numbers (`1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11`), advance by **one whole step** (two frets on your guitalele) at each mark:

* **1:** G
* **3:** A
* **5:** B
* **7:** C#
* **9:** D#
* **11:** F

### 3. Fill in the Even Positions (Whole Steps From C)

Next, move clockwise around the even numbers (`12, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10`), again advancing by **one whole step** at each mark:

* **12:** C
* **2:** D
* **4:** E
* **6:** F#
* **8:** G#
* **10:** A#

Once both passes are complete, every note on the Circle of Fifths is in its exact position.

| Clock Position | Note | Step Pattern |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **12** | **C** | *Even Anchor* |
| **1** | **G** | *Odd Anchor* |
| **2** | **D** | +1 whole step from C |
| **3** | **A** | +1 whole step from G |
| **4** | **E** | +1 whole step from D |
| **5** | **B** | +1 whole step from A |
| **6** | **F#** | +1 whole step from E |
| **7** | **C#** | +1 whole step from B |
| **8** | **G#** | +1 whole step from F# |
| **9** | **D#** | +1 whole step from C# |
| **10** | **A#** | +1 whole step from G# |
| **11** | **F** | +1 whole step from D# |

## Step 2: Extract Any Major Scale and Its Chords (Example: C# Major)

To see how the diagram works in practice, pick a complex key like **C# Major** (located at position `7`).

### 1. Find the 7 Notes of the Scale

Locate your root note (**C#** at `7`), step **one position counter-clockwise** (`6`: **F#**), and group **seven consecutive clockwise notes** together:

* **F#, C#, G#, D#, A#, F, C**

Arranged in alphabetical scale order from the root, those seven notes give you the **C# Major scale**: **C#, D#, F, F#, G#, A#, C** *(in strict classical notation, F and C are spelled enharmonically as E# and B#)*.

### 2. Map the Diatonic Chords (Major, Minor, and Diminished)

Those same seven consecutive notes automatically break down into the seven diatonic chords of the key in exact clockwise order:

* **Major Chords (I, IV, V) — The First 3 Notes (`6, 7, 8`):**
Your root note and its two immediate neighbors are always Major chords: **F# Major (IV)**, **C# Major (I)**, and **G# Major (V)**.
* **Minor Chords (ii, vi, iii) — The Next 3 Notes (`9, 10, 11`):**
The following three clockwise notes give you all three diatonic minor chords: **D# minor (ii)**, **A# minor (vi)**, and **F minor (iii)**.
* **Diminished Chord (vii°) — The 7th Note (`12`):**
The final note in your seven-note group is always diminished: **C diminished (vii°)**.

---

## Step 3: Find the Relative Minor in Three Steps

Every major key has a relative minor built from the **same seven notes and the same seven diatonic chords**. What changes is the tonal centre: the chords keep their shapes, but their Roman-numeral jobs shift. To find it on your clock diagram, start at your major root (**C#** at `7`) and count **three positions clockwise** (`7` $\rightarrow$ `8` $\rightarrow$ `9` $\rightarrow$ `10`):

* **Position 10:** **A# minor**

Any progression you build from the C# Major group (**F#, C#, G#, D#m, A#m, Fm, Cdim**) uses the very same notes and chords in **A# minor**—just relabelled around the new tonic: **A#m (i), Cdim (ii°), C# (III), D#m (iv), Fm (v), F# (VI), G# (VII)**. In fake books you may also meet this key as **B♭ minor**, the enharmonic spelling of the same seven notes.

Try sketching this 12-point clock before your next practice session and picking a random note on the wheel—within a minute, you'll have its full scale and chord palette mapped out without looking anything up.
